'''Tláhuac''' is one of the sixteen [[Boroughs of Mexico City|boroughs]] (''demarcación territorial'') of [[Mexico City]], located at the southeastern edge of the metropolitan area. Though Tláhuac still contains rural communities within its borders, mostly in the southern and eastern portions, the borough has undergone massive [[urbanization]] in the 20th century, especially in the northwest. Tláhuac has experienced the fastest rate of population growth in Mexico City since the 1960s.

Much of the borough's area is former lakebed where [[Lake Chalco]] and [[Lake Xochimilco]] met, with the town of San Pedro Tláhuac originally on an island. There are still some lake areas in the present day, along with four major canals and wetlands, also under conservation status. The urbanization has led to serious traffic and transportation problems. In 2012, Line 12 of the [[Mexico City Metro]] was completed with a terminal in the borough.

==Geography==
[[File:LagoReyesTlahuac.JPG|thumb|View of Lago de los Reyes Aztecas near San Juan Tláhuac]]
[[File:Cerro de Xico.JPG|thumb|View of the Xico mountain in Tláhuac]]
[[File:Tláhuac-hidro.png|thumb|Hydrodraulic map of the borough]]
The borough of Tláhuac is located in the southeastern part of Mexico City, about 60&nbsp;km from the [[historic center of Mexico City]] .<ref name="mexcity">{{cite web |url= http://www.mexicocity.gob.mx/contenido.php?cat=30900&sub=13|title= Tlahuác |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref><ref name="encmuc">{{cite web |url= http://www.e-local.gob.mx/work/templates/enciclo/EMM09DF/delegaciones/09011a.html |title= Tlahuác |year= 2010 |work= Enciclopedia de Los Municipios y Delegaciones de México Distrito Federal. |publisher= INAFED |location= Mexico |language= Spanish |accessdate= January 10, 2013 |url-status= dead |archiveurl= https://web.archive.org/web/20130601200012/http://www.e-local.gob.mx/work/templates/enciclo/EMM09DF/delegaciones/09011a.html |archivedate= June 1, 2013 }}</ref> It is bordered by the boroughs of [[Iztapalapa]], [[Milpa Alta]] and [[Xochimilco]], as well as by the municipality of [[Valle de Chalco Solidaridad]] in the [[State of Mexico]].<ref name="mexcity"/><ref name="encmuc"/> The borough extends over {{convert|89.5|sqkm|sqmi}} of territory, accounting for 5.75% of the area of the Federal District of Mexico City.<ref name="vsegura">{{cite web |url= http://www.unidad094.upn.mx/revista/51/08.html |author= Victor Manuel Segura Pérez |title= Diagnóstico de la deserción escolar en Tláhuac |work= Revista Xictli |publisher= Universidad Pegagógica Nacional |location=Mexico |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref><ref name="programa">{{cite web |url= https://www.ordenjuridico.gob.mx/Estatal/DISTRITO%20FEDERAL/Delegaciones/Tlahuac/TlaPro01.pdf |title= Programa Delegacional de Desarrollo Urbano de Tláhuac |publisher= Dirección General de Compilación y Consulta del Orden Jurídico SEGOB Nacional |location=Mexico |language=Spanish |trans-title=Borough program for urban development in Tlahuac|accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> The borough is on the eastern edge of the [[Valley of Mexico]], in the [[Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt]] that extends across central Mexico.<ref name="encmuc"/> The borough extends over much of the lakebeds of the former Lake Chalco and Lake Xochimilco where the two lakes were historically connected; the lakes were gradually drained over the centuries following the [[Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire|Spanish conquest]]. The territory of Tláhuac is classified into three zones: flat lakebed, transitional areas and hills with accumulated volcanic materials.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="programa"/> The main points of elevation include the Guadalupe Volcano, the Xaltepec Volcano, Tecuautzi, Tetecón, the Sierra de Santa Catarina, and the Teuhtli Volcano.<ref name="programa"/> The Sierra de Santa Catarina is a small volcanic belt which has elevations of up to 2,800 meters above sea level. It serves as an important aquifer recharge area as well as a barrier to [[urban sprawl]] from Iztapalapa on the other side.<ref name="programa"/> On the south end is the Tehuitli Volcano with an altitude of 2,700 meters above sea level.<ref name="encmuc"/>

The borough contains a number of neighborhoods and localities. Those considered by the government to be urban (with 2010 population figures in parentheses) are: Tláhuac (305,076), [[San Juan Ixtayopan]] (24,120), [[San Andrés Mixquic]] (13,310), [[Santa Catarina Yecahuitzotl]] (9,563), and [[San Nicolás Tetelco]] (4,246).<ref name=SEDESOL>{{cite web|title=Tláhuac|url=http://www.microrregiones.gob.mx/catloc/LocdeMun.aspx?tipo=clave&campo=loc&ent=09&mun=011|work=Catálogo de Localidades|publisher=[[Secretaría de Desarrollo Social]] (SEDESOL)|accessdate=17 April 2014}}</ref> In addition, the borough includes approximately 50 rural settlements, each with populations of less than 1,000.<ref name=SEDESOL/>

The predominant climate of the borough is semi-moist and temperate with an average annual temperature of {{convert|15.7|C|F}}, varying between a low of {{convert|8.3|C|F}} and a high of {{convert|22.8|C|F}}. The average annual rainfall is 533.8 mm, with most rain falling from June to August.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="programa"/>

Much of the area is former shallow lake with remaining open water, wetlands, and canals. Much of the surface water is saline due to the high mineral content of the soil and lack of natural outflow.<ref name="encmuc"/> The Lago de los Reyes Aztecas ("Lake of the Aztec Kings") covers 1.9 hectares and connects to the large network of canals and [[chinampa]]s in the borough. It hosts about twenty-five species of migratory birds, including ducks and storks, along with various fish, amphibians, and reptiles. It is the most important tourist attraction in the borough and has many docks and [[trajinera]] boats.<ref name="encmuc"/> The main docks in Tláhuac do not have a large number of boats or restaurants as in Xochimilco. It is still possible to see small natural islands as well as natural vegetation in much of the area, with chinampas still growing foodstuffs such as broccoli, chard, beets, chili peppers, peas, fava beans, and corn.<ref name="veredas">{{cite news |title= Veredas De Mexico / Las Chinampas Agricolas de Tlahuac |author=Antonio Suarez |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=May 28, 2000 |page=14 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Facts of Mexico/The agricultural chinampas of Tlahuac}}</ref> There are four main canals: Chalco, Guadalupano, Atecuyuac, and Amecameca. The first two are important as sources of water for the chinampas of the borough and as tourist attractions. There are also other smaller canals which serve as irrigation. On the border with the State of Mexico, there is an important ecological reserve lake area called the Cienega de Tláhuac. There is also an artificial lake at the Bosque de Tláhuac.<ref name="encmuc"/> Los Humedales wetlands, a remnant of Lake Chalco, is an ecological reserve which covers between 400 and 800 hectares depending on whether it is the rainy or dry season. It is one of the few wetlands left in the Valley of Mexico and hosts a large number of migratory birds. It is also home to species such as [[pocket gophers]], skunks, squirrels, snakes, and bats.<ref name="mexcity"/><ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="sanixtayo">{{cite web |url=http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=78:san-juan-ixtayopan&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58 |title=San Juan Ixtayopan |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |url-status=dead |archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20120412121446/http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=78:san-juan-ixtayopan&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58 |archivedate=April 12, 2012 }}</ref>

Much of the Tláhuac borough is an important recharge zone for aquifers, especially the area around the Sierra de Santa Catarina. There are a number of flood-prone areas, especially during the rainy season, mostly due to poor drainage systems and the existence of urban areas on former lakebed. Despite being built on a significant amount of former lakebed the borough in general is not prone to earthquake damage, with only a few areas with susceptibility scattered throughout the borough. There is also a fault line south of Avenida Tláhuac.<ref name="programa"/>

Most of the wild vegetation of the area is that of grassland with very little forest, mostly due to a long history of agriculture in the area. One of the few forested areas is the Ayaquemetl mountain near San Nicolás Tetelco, covering about ten hectares.<ref name="encmuc"/> There have been some efforts at reforestation.<ref name="20mil">{{cite news |title= Planta Tlahuac 20 mil arboles |author=Francisco Velazquez |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=July 30, 2000 |page=11 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Plant 20,000 trees in Tlahuac }}</ref> However, most of the plant life in the area is agricultural, especially in the east and south where corn, spinach, other leafy greens, [[alfalfa]], figs, pears, and walnuts are cultivated. There is also significant aquatic vegetation such as water lilies, chichicastle, and ninfa. Most of the wildlife of the area has been extinguished because of human encroachment, but in the Santa Catarina mountains there are still badgers, [[cacomistle]], and bats as well as various kinds of rodents and birds found throughout the borough.<ref name="encmuc"/>

The main problems confronting the borough, like others in the south of Mexico City, are urban sprawl and environmental concerns.<ref name="programa"/> The area is under transition from rural to urban as it borders the urbanized Iztapalapa and the rural Milpa Alta. Its population is growing as people move away from the more crowded city center.<ref name="encmuc"/>  33.5% of the territory is urbanized and 66.5% is classified as environmentally protected. Most of the latter land area (4,030 out of 5,674 hectares) is farmed. Most of the rest of the land corresponds to surface water such as the Ciénega de Tláhuac and the foothills of the Teuhtli mountains west of San Juan Ixtayopan. Just under 75% of the urbanized space is residential, with 12.1% mixed-use, 5.8% parks and green spaces, 5.5% occupied by irregular settlements, and 1.7% dedicated to urban infrastructure.<ref name="programa"/> The areas undergoing the most urbanization are La Nopalera, Agrícola Metropolitana, Villa Centroamericana, Santiago Norte, and La Asunción, mostly due to the construction of apartment buildings. Sprawl comes into the borough mostly from Iztapalapa, through Avenida Tláhuac and the San Rafael Atlixco rail line. It also comes in from Coyoacan and Xochimilco through the Canal de Chalco and [[Anillo Periférico]], as well as from Valle de Solidaridad Chalco in the State of Mexico. The best-preserved areas are those that border rural Milpa Alta. The main urban corridors are along Avenida Tláhuac, which is also where most of the commercial activity takes place. Other corridors include Avenida Estanislao Ramírez, Avenida La Turba, and Avenida Jiménez. Most of the mixed-use space is in the northwest of the borough and along Avenida Tláhuac. Urbanization is most prominent in the northwest of the borough, which has most of the area's apartment complexes. The rest of the borough has single-family homes and duplexes. Other areas with significant residential growth are Zapotitlán and San Francisco Tlaltenco which has led to a deterioration of their traditional rural appearance. This is especially true in locations closest to Avenida Tláhuac. Areas which have maintained more of their rural character are Santa Catarina Yechuizotl (sheltered by Sierra de Santa Catarina), as well as San Juan Ixtayopan, San Nicolás Tetelco and [[San Andrés Mixquic]], which are mostly surrounded by chinampas and other farmland that is under protection.<ref name="programa"/>

Other green spaces in the borough include parks, sporting venues, and recreational areas.<ref name="programa"/> The Bosque de Tláhuac is a recreational area with a number of attractions, which was opened in 2007, converted from a former landfill.<ref name="infantil">{{cite news |title= Hace Tláhuac playa menuda |author=Ariadna Bermeo |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=May 1, 2007 |page=2 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Tlahuac creates a small sized beach }}</ref> It extends over 72 hectares and features an artificial lake, a teaching farm/greenhouse, a cactus garden, volleyball and basketball courts, soccer fields, track, bicycle paths, and an area for cultural events.<ref name="encmuc"/> It also includes an area called the “Mini-Marquesa” with attractions such as ATV tracks similar to those found at the [[La Marquesa National Park]], as well as the city's first artificial beach (pools plus sand area) designed for children.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="infantil"/> The Parque de los Olivos dates back to the colonial period when in 1531 olive trees were planted here by missionaries and were some of the few that escaped destruction by royal decree to protect Spanish olive production. It also contains a small hill where archaeological artifacts from as far back as 400 BCE have been found.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="programa"/> Parque Xalli is located on the old road to San Francisco Tlaltenco. It was founded by Alejandro Reyes and Valentín Hernández with 3,000 square meters of installations related to ecology. It has a 60-meter-long zipline and a climbing wall. It has a large sand area for children, as well as a trampoline, pool, tree house, and mini-ziplines. There is also a petting zoo with various farm animals, most of which were donated to the park.<ref name="parquex">{{cite web |url=http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=805:parque-xalli-alternativa-de-esparcimiento-en-la-delegacion-tlahuac&catid=75:turismo-local&Itemid=58 |title=Parque Xalli, alternativa de esparcimiento en la Delegación Tláhuac |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |trans-title=Xalli Park, alternative to the sparseness of Tláhuac borough |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |url-status=dead |archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20120417081713/http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=805:parque-xalli-alternativa-de-esparcimiento-en-la-delegacion-tlahuac&catid=75:turismo-local&Itemid=58 |archivedate=April 17, 2012 }}</ref> Other green spaces include athletic and recreational centers in Santa Catarina, Colonia del Mar, Miguel Hidalgo, Zapotitlán, San Francisco Tlaltenco, San Pedro Tláhuac, San Juan Ixtayopan, San Nicolás Tetelco, and San Andrés Mixquic.<ref name="programa"/>

Pollution problems in the borough stem from urbanization of the area. Traffic congestion is a serious problem and parking space is severely limited. Much of the pollution in the borough comes from fixed sources along Avenida Tláhuac.<ref name="programa"/> Canal and lake waters have moderate problems with pollution in the way of garbage and the infestation of non-native water lily plants. The borough generates 356 tons of trash per day, 3.12% of the total produced in Mexico City. There are thirty areas where there is illegal house building, mostly near the Sierra de Santa Catarina and around San Juan Ixtayopan and La Conchita.<ref name="programa"/>

==Seven Native Towns==
[[File:ParishSanPedroTlahuac 03.JPG|thumb|San Pedro Apóstol church in San Pedro Tláhuac]]
The most important communities in the borough are called the Seven Original/Native Towns of Tláhuac (Siete Pueblos Originarios de Tláhuac), meaning that they were established before the arrival of the Spanish. These are Santiago Zapotitlán, San Francisco Tlaltenco, Santa Catarina Yechuizotl, San Nicolas Tetelco, San Juan Ixtayopan, San Andrés Mixquic and San Pedro Tláhuac.<ref name="programa"/>

San Pedro Tláhuac is the oldest of these, founded in the 13th century. From that time until well into the colonial period, it was an island in the waters between Lake Chalco and Lake Xochimilco, and was the capital of a dominion that stretched of much of what is now the borough of Tláhuac. After the Spanish conquest, the ceremonial center was destroyed on top was built a church and monastery dedicated to [[Saint Peter]], dated to 1529.<ref name="encmuc"/> At the time, it was one of the most important churches in the southern Valley of Mexico. It was rebuilt several times due to moisture damage with the current structure dating to the 17th century.<ref name="programa"/><ref name="sanpedro">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=51:san-pedro-tlahuac&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58|title= San Pedro, Tláhuac |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> The San Pedro church as three naves with images of Saint Peter, [[Saint Joachim]] and the [[Holy Trinity]] inside. In the tower there is a clock dating to 1924, which was donated by the Ejido of Tláhuac.<ref name="programa"/> Around the town, there is an areas with chinampas and a small residual lake called the Lago de los Reyes Aztecas, which also hosts the “Museo Vivo” (Live Museum) about the canals and farming of the area. Its main festivity is the Regional Fair of Tláhuac, held at the end of June along with honors to the town's patron saint.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="sanpedro"/>
[[File:Alumbrada7Mixquic.JPG|thumb|View of the "alumbrada" event for Day of the Dead at San Andrés Mixquic]]
[[San Andrés Mixquic]] in the [[Mesoamerica]]n period was also a dominion located on a small lake island. Its main landmark is its parish church, founded in 1537.<ref name="encmuc"/> This church was built on top of a sanctuary for the goddess of life and death with archeological excavations finding many artifacts including a [[Chac Mool]] and two rings for a [[Mesoamerican ball court]].<ref name="anmix">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=76:san-andres-mixquic&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58|title= San Andrés Mixquic |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> The original building was mostly destroyed in an earthquake and then rebuilt in 1600. The only remains from the first church is the bell tower, which is separate from the church building. In each of the town's named neighborhoods, there is a stone cross, which is the center of celebrations on May 3. The patron saint is honored on November 30, with processions. San Andrés Mixquis is known for its [[Day of the Dead]] celebrations which attract many visitors each year.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="anmix"/>

San Francisco Tlaltenco is one of the largest communities in the borough, located in the north of the borough on the south side of the Sierra de Santa Catarina. It main church is the San Francisco de Asís founded in 1547. Another important landmark is the Gate or Arch of Tlaltenco, which dates to the colonial period. It served as a customs checkpoint until just after the Mexican War of Independence. It is considered to be symbolic of the area. During the [[Mexican Revolution]], it was occupied by the [[Liberation Army of the South|Zapatistas]] and those loyal to [[Venustiano Carranza]] as a campsite. Its patron saint, [[Francis of Assisi]], is honored for eight days starting on October 4 with processions and [[sawdust carpet]]s. Another major celebration is Carnival which is celebrated with [[Chinelos]]. On the fifth Sunday after [[Ash Wednesday]], a Christ figure called the Señor de Mazatepec is honored.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="orilla">{{cite web |url=http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=77:san-francisco-tlaltenco-qa-la-orilla-de-la-tierraq&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58 |title=San Francisco Tlaltenco: "A la Orilla de la Tierra" |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |trans-title=San Francisco Tlaltenco: "At the edge of the land" |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |url-status=dead |archiveurl=https://web.archive.org/web/20120412124732/http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=77:san-francisco-tlaltenco-qa-la-orilla-de-la-tierraq&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58 |archivedate=April 12, 2012 }}</ref>
[[File:ChurchSantiagoZapotitlán01.JPG|thumb|left|View of the parish church in Santiago Zapotitlán]]
Santiago Zapotitlán was founded in 1435. After the Aztecs conquered the area, they rearranged the layout of this town. After the Spanish conquest, the Inmaculada Concepción church was established in 1541 and rebuilt in 1939. Its founding is celebrated on December 8 of each year. Santiago Zapotitlán is noted for its religious and cultural traditions with both pre Hispanic and Spanish roots. One of its main celebrations is the La Fiesta de Luces y Música (Festival of Lights and Music) which is based on the [[New Fire ceremony]]. Today it is celebrated with traditional dances and fireworks along with music played on both indigenous and European instruments.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="szap">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=81:santiago-zapotitlan&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58 |title= Santiago Zapotitlán |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref>

San Juan Ixtayopan is in the south of the borough made up of five named neighborhoods called San Augustin, La Concepción, La Soledad, La Asunción and La Lupita. Just outside there are six others called Francisco Villa, El Rosario, Tierra Blanca, Peña Alta and Jardines del Llano. Its main church is La Soledad constructed in the 19th century. Although it has been severely affected urban sprawl, there is still some chinampa farming surrounding it.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="sanixtayo"/>  The town was the site of the Battles of Ixtayopan during the Mexican Revolution. Zapatistas under Everardo González took over the area from federal troops with the support of the local people. Later these forces battled those loyal to Venustiano Carranza in the power struggles that ensued among the rebel factions.<ref name="sanixtayo"/> San Juan Ixtayopan has a site museum located in Los Olivos Park which houses a number of archeological finds with 188 exhibited. The main plaza is the Plaza de la Soledad, built in the 17th century. The main festivals are for Our Lady of Solitude from 2–5 January and for [[John the Baptist]] from 22 – 24 June. The town is noted for a hot beverage called calientito” (little hot one) which is a mix of sugar cane alcohol, orange juice, [[guava]], raisins and sugar cane.<ref name="sanixtayo"/> In August, San Juan Ixtayopan hosts an annual Corn Fair to promote products made with the grain as well as the local culture.<ref name="feriaelote">{{cite news |title= Feria del elote en Tláhuac|author=Josefina Quintero |url= http://radiotrece.com.mx/feria-del-elote-en-tlahuac/ |newspaper=Radio Trece |location=Mexico City |date=July 30, 2012 |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Corn Fair in Tláhuac }}</ref>
[[File:Tapete de aserrín en la calle 20 de Noviembre, en San Juan Ixtayopan.jpg|thumb|Sawdust carpet created in San Juan Ixtayopan]]
San Nicolás Tetelco is centered on its parish church dedicated to [[Nicholas of Tolentino]], constructed in the 17th century. In the presbytery, there is a large crucifix which is probably made from corn stalk in the colonial era as well as a holy water font from the 16th century.<ref name="nictet">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=79:san-nicolas-tetelco-hacinamiento-de-piedras&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58|title= San Nicolás Tetelco: Hacinamiento de piedras |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |trans-title=San Nicolás Tetlco:Heap of stones |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> Other landmarks include the Plaza Emiliano Zapata, which is surrounded by older constructions, the Monument to Christ the King, the Ahuapa sports facility, the Ayaquemetl ecological area and the chinampa zones. It is also home to the former hacienda Santa Fe, which was founded in the 17th century which operated until the Mexican Revolution. Remains of its chapel, main house and workers’ living quarters still remain.  The town is noted for its [[atole]], cooked traditionally over a wood fire.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="nictet"/>
[[File:Yecahuízotl-Plaza del pueblo.JPG|thumb|left|Plaza in Santa Catarina Yecahuitzotl]]
Santa Catarina Yecahuitzotl is located in the northeast of the borough. Its church, dedicated to Saint Catherine, dates to 1647, constructed of [[tezontle]]. The atrium has four small chapels located in the four corners.<ref name="encmuc"/> Until the 1960s, the community was isolated from Mexico City. However at that time urban sprawl began to reach it changing much of the community's way of life. Its original name was Acatzinco, then Santa Catarina Cuautli-Itlacuayan. Its current name has been interpreted to mean “the third part of the south road,” the “nose of an amphibian” and “land of ants.”<ref name="sancatyec">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=80:santa-catarina-yecahuizotl&catid=35:pueblos-de-tlahuac&Itemid=58|title= Santa Catarina Yecahuizotl |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> The name change came with the town's refounding in 1924 when [[ejido]] members organized their lands among themselves. Other important locations include La Troje, site of Mexican Revolution activity, the [[charrería]] ring, the former Xico hacienda and two environmental education centers. It is divided into a number of neighborhoods called colonias such as Maria Isabel, Santiago, Del Carmen, San Juan Tlalpizahuac, Ampliación San Juan TlalpizahuacSan Francisco Apolocalco, La Cañada, Campestre Potrero, San Francisco Tlaltenco, Selene and Ampliacion Selene.<ref name="sancatyec"/>

==History==
[[File:Idolo encontrado en la zona chinanpera de Mixquic.JPG|thumb|Brazier from the San Andrés Mixquic area]]
The name “Tláhuac” comes from [[Nahuatl]], mostly likely a derivation of “Cuitláhuac”. However, its meaning is in dispute, with interpretations including “the place where cuítatl (a type of algae) is gathered”, “place where the Lord sings”, “guardian or steward of the land", “born among the rocks of the lakes” and “place of he who cares for the water”.<ref name="mexcity"/><ref name="programa"/>

The Tláhuac was established on a small island in the waters between Lake Chalco and Lake Xochimilco in 1222 by the [[Chichimeca]]s. By the 14th century, it had become a dominion expanding as far as what is now Santiago Zapotitlán and Santa Catarina Yecahuizotl, but distinct from the dominion of Mixquic, which was on a nearby island. Tláhuac was connected to the mainland by two causeways leading to Santiago Zapotitlán and Tulyehualco.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="programa"/> The first regional leader of the area was Cohuatomatzin in 1262. The dominion remained independent until the 14th century, when it was conquered by the [[Tepanec]]s. Soon after, it was dominated by the [[Aztec]]s. During the pre-Hispanic period, the economy of the area was based on chinampa farming and fishing. After conquest, the area paid tribute to [[Azcapotzalco (altepetl)|Azcapotzalco]] and then to [[Tenochtitlan]].<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="nostalgia">{{cite news |title= Ciudad de la Nostalgia/ Fiesta en Tlahuac |author=Alberto Barranco Chavarria |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=July 5, 1998 |page=7 |language=Spanish |trans-title=City of Nostalgia/Festival in Tlahuac}}</ref> The town of Tláhuac was laid out along the various dikes and canals which centered on a ceremonial center, where the San Pedro parish church is today.<ref name="programa"/>

The area was prone to flooding but it was prosperous due to intensive chinampa farming, growing corn, beans, [[chili pepper]]s, tomatoes, squash, flowers and more.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="programa"/> Much of this produce was sent to Mexico City as a way of paying tribute. There was a major flood in 1499, prompting the construction of a large dike against the lake waters. Another dike was constructed to separate the lakes.<ref name="nostalgia"/>

After the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire, colonial authorities defined Tláhuac as extending to Zapotitlán, Cuauhtlitl-Tlacuayan (Santa Catarina) and Tulyehualco.<ref name="programa"/> In the colonial period, the Spanish authorities initially emphasized Mixquic as a political center because it has only one indigenous chief, while Tláhuac was divided under four. Three orders began evangelical work, the [[Augustinians]] in Mixquic and the [[Franciscans]], replaced by the [[Dominican Order|Dominicans]] in Tláhuac.<ref name="programa"/> By the 18th century, Tláhuac had come under the administration of Chalco.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="nostalgia"/> The area's economy remain tied to agriculture, still sending much of the production to Mexico City, now to be sold. Flood control projects continued, with drainage of lake area begun in 1555.<ref name="programa"/><ref name="nostalgia"/> During the colonial period and into the 19th century, transportation by small boats was primary on the area's lakes and canals. Boats transported not only people but cargo, especially agricultural produce bound for Mexico City.<ref name="programa"/> Customs gates were installed on the main canals in Tlatenco and Tulyehualco to collect taxes on various vegetables, cereals and alcohol from the state of Morelos. Important colonial era haciendas included San Nicolas Tolentino, Santa Fe Tetelco and Zoquiapan.<ref name="nostalgia"/>

After the [[Mexican War of Independence]], Tláhuac became part of the State of Mexico. When the Federal District of Mexico City was expanded in 1854, Tláhuac became part of the District, then part of the prefecture of Xochimilco in 1857.<ref name="encmuc"/> Agriculture remained the main economic activity. In the late 19th century, the Santa Fe hacienda was known for its cattle and the size of the vegetables grown there.<ref name="nostalgia"/> In 1856, the Mexico City-Tlahuac causeway flooded completely. In 1895, a project was started to drain Lake Chalco. Soon after, the Chalco-Tlaltenco road was constructed over the former lake bed, followed by a rail line that passed through half of the current territory of the borough.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="nostalgia"/> In the latter 19th century, steamships traveling between Mexico City and Chalco passed by.<ref name="nostalgia"/>

In the 19th century, the town of Tláhuac had municipality status which was eliminated in 1903 with a reorganization of the Federal District.<ref name="encmuc"/> The [[Mexican Revolution]] soon followed with one major battle at San Juan Ixtayopan between forces loyal to [[Emiliano Zapata]] and [[Venustiano Carranza]].<ref name="programa"/> After the end of the military conflict, residents petitioned for autonomy, leading to the segregation of Tláhuac from Xochimilco in 1924 as a municipality. In 1928, this revived municipality was reorganized again into the current borough.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="sanpedro"/> The borders of the borough were not finalized until 1994, over opposition from Ejido members in some communities along the border with the State of Mexico.<ref name="programa"/>

The urban sprawl of Mexico City began to take over the [[Ejido]] lands in the mid-20th century, with former farmlands converted into housing subdivisions, especially in the areas closest to Iztapalapa.<ref name="programa"/>

In November 2004, three policemen were [[2004 Tláhuac lynching|surrounded by an angry mob and lynched]] in San Juan Ixtayopan. They were accused of being kidnappers after taking pictures of children. Two of the policemen were burned alive while one rescued hours after the incident began.<ref name=":4">{{Cite news |last1=C. McKinley Jr |first1=James |last2=Thompson |first2=Ginger |date=2004-11-25 |title=Lynchings of Policemen Ignite Outrage at Violence in Mexico |url=https://www.nytimes.com/2004/11/25/world/americas/lynchings-of-policemen-ignite-outrage-at-violence-in-mexico.html |url-access=subscription |url-status=live |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20170909124207/https://www.nytimes.com/2004/11/25/world/americas/lynchings-of-policemen-ignite-outrage-at-violence-in-mexico.html |archive-date=9 September 2017 |access-date=2024-07-07 |work=The New York Times |language=en-US}}</ref><ref name=":2">{{Cite web |last=Hernández |first=Bertha |date=2023-02-25 |title=Linchamiento: la oscura noche en Tláhuac |trans-title=Lynching: the dark night in Tláhuac |url=https://www.cronica.com.mx/nacional/linchamiento-oscura-noche-tlahuac.html |access-date=2024-07-07 |website=Cronica |language=es}}</ref>

==Culture==
[[File:OfrendaColonMixquic.JPG|thumb|Large altar/offering to the dead at a primary school in San Andrés Mixquic]]
The area considers itself traditional conserving many colonial and Mesoamerican era traditions.<ref name="encmuc"/> Culturally the most important communities are the seven which have pre Hispanic roots which were located on the banks of Lakes Chalco and Xochimilco. Today these communities area marked by the oldest churches in the borough.<ref name="encmuc"/><ref name="programa"/> Other important heritage sites as demarcated by [[INAH]] and the borough include the chinampas, located in various parts of the borough and Parque de los Olivos.<ref name="programa"/>

One important tradition is [[Day of the Dead]], celebrated throughout the borough but San Andrés Mixquic's is particularly well known, attracting thousands of visitors from Mexico and abroad.<ref name="mexcity"/><ref name="encmuc"/> The celebration generally lasts three days from October 31 to November 1 each day focusing on different groups, children, adults and saints. One local tradition related to the day is to hang a paper lantern often in the form of a star at the entrance to home as a way to greet the return of the souls of the dead.<ref name="encmuc"/> The Fiestas del Santo Jubileo is a custom observed on different days in the communities of the borough. It consists of a mass and exhibition of the [[Sacramental bread|Host]] on the main altar of the church for 40 hours, surrounded by flowers, sawdust carpets, live music, dances and fireworks.<ref name="encmuc"/> Carnival is important in San Francisco Tlaltenco and Santiago Zapotitlán, where dancers generally are chinelos and those in [[charro]] suits. These carnivals begin on the fourth Sunday of Lent. The tradition in San Francisco Tlaltenco goes back to the end of the 19th century.<ref name="encmuc"/> A reenactment of [[Palm Sunday]] has been held each year at San Pedro Tláhuac for over eighty years, with the procession winding its way through eight different neighborhoods.<ref name="laspalmas">{{cite news |title= Celebran en Tlahuac a 'senor de las palmas' |author=Luz Romano |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=March 25, 2002 |page=7 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Celebrate the Lord of the Palms in Tlahuac }}</ref> San Pedro Tláhuac is also the site of the Regional Fair of Tláhuac. It was first organized in 1956, with the goal so attracting tourism and encouraging youth to maintain traditions. It begins with a procession of the image of the patron saint, the Apostle Peter, in decorated trajinera boats in the chinampa areas of Lago de los Reyes Aztecas. Other events include the selection of a fair queen, conferences, cultural events, concerts, dances, fireworks and more, with proceeds going to the restoration of the San Pedro Apostal Church.<ref name="pelea">{{cite news |title= Pelea Tlahuac sus tradiciones |author=Nadia Sanders |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=June 21, 2004 |page=7 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Tlahuac fights for its traditions }}</ref>
The Baile del Guajolote (Dance of the Turkey) is a traditional dance in the towns of Santiago Zapotitlán and San Pedro Tláhuac, which is performed at weddings, baptism, first communions and quinceañeras. It includes the gifting of a live turkey along with a large food basket from one family to another. Another important traditional dance, especially in Santiago Zapotitlan is the Dance of Moors and Christians also called the Danza de los Santiagueros (Dance of those of Saint James).  The dance represents the battles between the Moors and Christians in which [[Saint James the Moor-slayer|Saint James]] plays a central role.<ref name="encmuc"/>

Typical foods are mostly similar to those found in the rest of Mexico City. One local dish is michmole, made from fish and sauce with herbs such as [[epazote]] .<ref name="encmuc"/> 
[[File:Faro de Tláhuac-éxodos ambulantes.jpg|thumb|Exhibition outside FARO-Tláhuac]]
Overall, there is a significant lack of cultural institutions such as theaters, auditoriums and museums. However a number of institutions do exist.<ref name="programa"/> The Tlahuac Regional Museum is located in San Pedro Tláhuac. It is housed in the former civil registry office and was the first museum established in the borough.<ref name="musreg">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=1231:museo-regional-tlahuac&catid=107:museos-tlahuac&Itemid=57|title= Museo Regional Tláhuac |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> The Regional Museum of Mixquic is on the side of the parish church. It contains exhibitions of local archeological pieces which mostly date between 1200 and 1500CE but also some from the [[Toltec]] and [[Teotihuacan]] periods. The church and former monastery are also classed as an archeological site.<ref name="encmuc"/> The Andrés Quintana Roo Museum has a collection of 279 archeological pieces from the collectionof Dr. Socorro Bernal Roque. The pieces are mostly of clay and stone from the Teotihuacan era found in the Tlahuac area. The most important pieces are a chac-mool, the remains of a teotlachtli, clay and stone seals, a [[tzompantli]] and a cylindrical serpent.<ref name="musroo">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=1233:museo-andres-quintanaroo&catid=107:museos-tlahuac&Itemid=57|title= Museo Andrés Quintana Roo |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> The Tomás Medina Villarruel Museum is named after a native of San Juan Ixtayopan who is an important figure in the conservation of the archeological record of the area. In 1984, he and his family donated a collection of 850 pieces to the borough. The museum was built in 1998 as a community museum and the collection has since grown with new donations.<ref name="mustomas">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=1232:museo-qtomas-medina-villaruelq&catid=107:museos-tlahuac&Itemid=57|title= Museo "Tomás Medina Villaruel" |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> The Fábrica de Artes y Oficios FARO Tláhuac was founded in 2006, as part of a network of FAROs sponsored by the city Secretary of Culture. Its aim is to promote community involvement in the arts and various trades. It has workshops on various to allow residents to learn creative and practical skills as well as conferences, cinema, theater, art exhibitions, concerts and more.<ref name="farot">{{cite web |url= http://www.tlahuac.df.gob.mx/index.php?option=com_content&view=article&id=1242:faro-tlahuac&catid=108:casas-de-cultura-en-tlahuac&Itemid=57|title= Faro Tláhuac |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref><ref name="culdf">{{cite web |url= http://www.cultura.df.gob.mx/index.php/recintos/faros/faro-de-tlahuac |title= Fábrica de Artes y Oficios Tlahuác |publisher=Borough of Tláhuac |location=Mexico City |language=Spanish |accessdate=January 10, 2013 }}</ref> Other cultural institutions in the borough include the Centro Cultural Zapotitlán, Centro Cultural Santa Catarina, Centro Cultural Nopalera, Casa de Cultura Diego Rivera, Casa de Cultura Frida Kahlo, Casa de Cultura Ampliación Los Olivos and the Casa de Cultura Rosario Castellanos.

==Socioeconomics==
[[File:Tláhuac-Zonas urbanas y vialidades.svg|thumb|Maps depicting urbanized areas in the borough]]
From the second half of the 20th century, the population and number of housing units has risen rapidly.<ref name="programa"/> The population has grown from 29,880 inhabitants in 1960, to 146,293 in 1980, to 255,891 in 1995, to 361,014 in 2010,<ref name="vsegura"/><ref name="programa"/> to 392,313 as of the 2020 census.<ref name="Data México"/> In the 1960s and 1970s, the rate of growth was over 7%. It has since slowed to under 4.5%, but it is still the highest in the Federal District. The main draw is the relatively low cost of land in comparison to the rest of the city. Tláhuac has a population density of 90 inhabitants per hectare, one of the lowest in the Federal District, which has an average of 128 per hectare. However, in some areas, density has climbed to over 200 per hectare mostly in the northwest. However, most of the borough still has fewer than 100 per hectare. Neighborhoods with the densest population include La Nopalera, Colonial Miguel Hidalgo and Colonial Los Olivos where most of the borough's apartment complexes can be found. Just under eighty percent of housing units are owned by their occupants, but still about ninety percent of housing units are single family homes.<ref name="programa"/>

Between 60,000 and 70,000 families inhabit 93 irregular settlements in Tlahuac.<ref name="daño">{{cite news |title= Detectan daño a suelo verde en Tláhuac |author=Pablo León |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=March 24, 2010 |page=6 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Detect damage to green areas in Tláhuac}}</ref> While some of the irregular settlements are in conservation areas, most are in areas in which the topography does not allow for the provision of urban services such as water. Although basic services extend to over 80-90 percent of the population, communities in the periphery and those which are new and not yet consolidated tend to suffer a lack of basic services along with transportation and roads.<ref name="programa"/>

Just under seventy percent of the population is under age thirty but this percentage has been slowing shrinking as the borough ages.<ref name="programa"/>

The Consejo Nacional de Población ranks Tláhuac as having a very low level of socioeconomic marginalization. About seventy percent of the population is employed in commerce and services. Twenty eight percent is involved in manufacturing and about 3.5% work in agriculture, the highest percentage in the Federal District. Over ninety percent of the commerce in the delegation occurs in small businesses, mostly along Avenida Tláhuac, Avenida La Turba and in the centers of the towns. Informal commerce, especially [[tianguis]] markets, is found in Zapotitlpan, San Pedro Tláhuac, near the San Lorenzo Tezonco cemetery and some along Avenida Tláhuac. There are 712 manufacturing concerns generally small concerns located in the northwest of the borough, producing food products, drinks, tobacco products, metal products, machinery and equipment. The service sector is mostly related to repair and maintenance. Most agriculture is found in the south and east with just over 4,000 hectares are under cultivation in the borough growing corn, animal feed, spinach, alfalfa and figs. Most of the livestock raised is pigs, followed by cattle than sheep.<ref name="programa"/>

The borough has two groups of police and five monitoring stations.<ref name="programa"/> However, the urbanization of the area has brought in increased crime including gang activity.<ref name="pandillas">{{cite news |title= Quieren reinsertar a pandilleros de Tláhuac |author=Victor Adrian Espinosa |url= http://www.eluniversaldf.mx/tlahuac/nota54933.html|newspaper=El Universal |location=Mexico City |date=December 26, 2012 |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |language=Spanish |trans-title=They want to work with gang members in Tlahuac }}</ref>

==Education==
The borough has sixty seven preschools, seventy nine primary schools, twenty three middle schools and four high schools. There are sufficient primary and middle schools but a lack of educational services above this level. There are public libraries in Santiago Zapotitlán, San Pedro Tláhuac, Colonial del Mar, Miguel Hidalgo and Tetelco.<ref name="programa"/> The Universidad Insurgentes has several installations in the borough.  The borough has a 5.61% illiteracy rate which compares favorably to the rest of Mexico City, but drop out rates are a concern. Most students drop out at the high school level or above, completing primary and middle school. But even at the primary level the dropout rate is 22.8%. The primary reason is the financial situation of the family.<ref name="vsegura"/><ref name="programa"/>

Public high schools of the ''[[Instituto de Educación Media Superior del Distrito Federal]]'' (IEMS) include:<ref>"[http://www.iems.df.gob.mx/tlahuac_303-12.html Planteles Tláhuac]." Instituto de Educación Media Superior del Distrito Federal. Retrieved on May 28, 2014.</ref>

==Transportation==
[[File:MetroTlahuac02.JPG|thumb|View of the Tláhuac terminal and bus stop]]
Transportation for the borough connects the area with neighboring boroughs and the State of Mexico. The Most important road is Avenida Tláhuac, which crosses almost the entire territory, followed by Anillo Periférico and the Mexico City-Tulyehualco road.<ref name="programa"/><ref name="mexcity"/> Road traffic is heavy and congested during many hours of the day, including weekends. Some of the worst traffic in the borough is in Santiago Tulyehualco, where various major roads such as Aquiles Serdan, Francisco Presa, the Tlahuac-Tulyehualco road and Isidro Tapia all converge.<ref name="colapsan">{{cite news |title=Vehículos "colapsan" Tlahuac por L12 |author=Josué Huerta |url= http://www.eluniversal.com.mx/ciudad/114564.html |newspaper=El Universal |location=Mexico City |date=December 11, 2012 |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Vehicles “collapse” Tlahuac because of Line 12 }}</ref>
There are both public and private bus lines, mostly working out of four official bus stations. There is also one taxi station but there are also a large number of informal bicycle taxis, motorcycle taxis and taxis converted from golf carts because of the lack of organized public transportation in a number of areas.<ref name="programa"/><ref name="dertp">{{cite news |title= Ruta exprés de RTP conectará a Tláhuac con la Línea 12 |author=Josefina Quintero |url= https://www.jornada.unam.mx/2012/11/14/capital/042n3cap |newspaper=La Jornada |location=Mexico City |date=November 14, 2012 |page=42 |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |language=Spanish |trans-title=RTP express route will connect to Tlahuac with Line 12 }}</ref>

A major change in public transportation is the completion of Line 12 of the [[Mexico City Metro]], which a terminal in Tláhuac. The construction of the line into the borough was controversial. One argument was that it would worsen the already bad traffic congestion by encouraging more urban sprawl.<ref name="colapsan"/> Ejido members protested the appropriation of the Ejido of San Francisco Tlaltenco in order to build the terminal.<ref name="desalojan">{{cite news |title= Desalojan en Tláhuac a opositores al Metro |author=Jonás López and Ivan Sosa |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=April 4, 2009 |page=1 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Removed those opposing the Metro from Tlahuac }}</ref> The establishment of the terminal Tláhuac caused a struggle between bus operators in the Federal District and neighboring State of Mexico for rights to transport passengers between the station and points in the State of Mexico, especially the suburb of Chalco.<ref name="pelean">{{cite news |title="Pelean" rutas espacios en Tláhuac |author=Josué Huerta |url= http://www.eluniversal.com.mx/ciudad/113700.html |newspaper=El Universal |location=Mexico City |date=October 9, 2012 |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Bus routes “fight” for space in Tláhuac }}</ref><ref name="quiebra">{{cite news |title=Temen "quiebra" por rutas de Edomex en Tláhuac |author=Josué Huerta |url= http://www.eluniversal.com.mx/ciudad/113715.html |newspaper=El Universal |location=Mexico City |date=October 9, 2012 |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Bus routes “fight” for space in Tláhuac }}</ref> Residents complained that construction of the line left Avenida Tláhuac in poor condition for local traffic and pedestrians.<ref name="destrulin">{{cite news |title= Obras de la línea 12 destruyeron avenida Tláhuac |author=Josefina Quintero |url= https://www.jornada.unam.mx/2012/08/27/capital/040n2cap |newspaper=La Jornada |location=Mexico City |date=August 27, 2012 |page=40 |accessdate=January 10, 2013 |language=Spanish |trans-title=Work on Line 12 destroyed Avenida Tlahuac }}</ref>
