'''Canudos'''  is a [[Municipalities of Brazil|municipality]] in the northeast region of [[Bahia]], [[Brazil]]. The original town, since flooded by the [[Cocorobó Dam]], was the scene of violent [[War of Canudos|clashes]] between peasants and republican police in the 1890s.

The municipality contains part of the [[Raso da Catarina]] ecoregion.<ref>{{citation|ref={{harvid|Lampião e Conselheiro no Raso da Catarina – Bahia!}}|language=pt|title=Lampião e Conselheiro no Raso da Catarina|work=Bahia!|url=http://bahia.com.br/roteiros/lampiao-e-conselheiro-no-raso-da-catarina/|access-date=2016-11-03|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20161023185136/http://bahia.com.br/roteiros/lampiao-e-conselheiro-no-raso-da-catarina/|archive-date=2016-10-23|url-status=dead}}</ref>

==History==

The town of Canudos was founded in the racially diverse<ref>Da Cunha, Euclides: "Rebellion in the Backlands", Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1944, p. 67.</ref> [[Bahia]] state of northeastern [[Brazil]] in 1893 by Antônio Vicente Mendes Maciel, an itinerant [[preacher]] from [[Ceara]].<ref>Della Cava, Ralph: "Brazilian Messianism and National Institutions: A Reappraisal of Canudos and Joaseiro", 406. The Hispanic American Historical Review, vol. 48, no. 3, Aug. 1968, p. 406</ref> Mendes Maciel had been wandering through the backroads and lesser-inhabited areas of the country from the 1870s onwards, followed by a band of loyal supporters. As his following swelled, he took on the name [[Antônio Conselheiro]] (''Antônio the Counselor'') and increasingly began to trouble the local authorities, who saw him as a Monarchist<ref>Madden, Lori: "The Canudos War in History", Luso-Brazilian Review, vol. 30, no. 2, Special Issue: "The World Out of Which Canudos Came". Winter 1993, pp. 5,6.</ref> and thus a threat to their legitimacy.

===Settlement===
In 1893, following a protest over [[taxation]] and a violent melee with the police forces in Masseté, Conselheiro and his band settled on an abandoned farm called Canudos, so called because a plant, ''canudo-de-pita'' (scientific name ''[[Ipomoea carnea]]'', its popular name referring to its hollow tubes, used for manufacturing smoking pipes) was common in the region. The place was named ''Belo Monte'' (Beautiful Mount) by Antonio Conselheiro, but the old name, ''Arraial de Canudos'', prevailed. Over the years people from across Bahia, including landless [[farmer]]s, former [[slavery|slaves]], [[Indigenous people of Brazil|indigenous people]] and [[Cangaço|''cangaceiros'']] flocked to join him, and within a few years the fledgling settlement numbered 30,000 people (which made it the second largest urban center in Bahia behind Salvador<ref name="Levine, Robert M. 1991">Levine, Robert M. "Canudos in the National Context." ''The Americas'' (Oct., 1991), 207.</ref>) and had developed a leather exporting business.<ref>Madden, Lori: "The Canudos War in History", Luso-Brazilian Review, vol. 30, no. 2, Special Issue: "The World Out of Which Canudos Came". Winter 1993, p. 7.</ref>

As a community, Canudos operated somewhat like a religious commune, with Antônio Conselheiro as the principal member and director. Canudos was a heavily religious settlement, under the sway of Antonio's fanaticism, but despite his fanaticism he did not assume any official position of authority. The settlement practiced common ownership, abolished the official currency, negated Brazilian national laws and participated collectively in the management of the town. Canudos was in essence a reaction against the contemporary Brazilian nation.<ref>Da Cunha, Euclides: "Rebellion in the Backlands", Chicago: University of Chicago Press, 1944</ref>

Neither the local nor national government supported the settlement in Canudos. The local government of Bahia felt pressure from landowners to take action against the settlement because of labor shortages caused by migration.<ref name="Levine, Robert M. 1991"/> The Brazilian national government wanted a military expedition sent to destroy Canudos in the name of liberalism and progress.<ref>Johnson, Adriana. “The War of the End of the World or the End of Ideology.” ''Journal of Latin American Cultural Studies'' (August, 2004). 223</ref> In the words of one historian, "The mere existence of autonomous movements not subject to state control was antithetical to the national interest. Canudos stood for such autonomy, and therefore had to be destroyed."<ref name="Levine, Robert M. 1991"/>

===Suppression of the community===
{{main|War of Canudos}}
The first three invasions were amply defeated by the villagers. However, in 1897, a considerably larger fourth invasion force managed to overwhelm the village. Their success was in part helped by the death, from [[dysentery]], of Antônio Conselheiro, during the early stages of the [[siege]]. The [[Military of Brazil|Brazilian army]] showed no mercy, brutally massacring the survivors and destroying the entire village.  An academic, Alvim Horcades, would thus describe the massacre:

"Eu vi e assisti a sacrificar-se todos aqueles miseráveis (...) e com sinceridade o digo: em Canudos foram degolados quase todos os prisioneiros (...) Arrancar-se a vida a uma criancinha (...) é o maior dos barbarismos e dos crimes que o homem pode praticar." ("I saw and witnessed the sacrifice of all those poor people (...) and I say with all sincerity: in Canudos almost all the prisoners were beheaded (...) To take the life of a little child (...) is the greatest of cruelties and crimes man can commit."){{Citation needed|date=April 2023}}

==Post-War Canudos==
Today the area is submerged by water, the result of the [[Cocorobó Dam]] project in the 1970s, which blocked the [[Vaza-Barris River]] and flooded the old city.  At low water the ruins of the [[church (building)|church]] that was once the village's centrepiece can occasionally be seen.  Once a year, in October, a [[mass (liturgy)|mass]] is held to commemorate those lost in what is known today as the [[War of Canudos]].  The [[municipality]] of Nova Canudos was built nearby, at [[latitude]] 09°53'48" South and [[longitude]] 39°01'35" West, and currently has around 13,000 inhabitants.

The story of Canudos was told by [[war correspondent]] [[Euclides da Cunha]] in the book ''[[Os Sertões]]'' (1901; translated into English as ''Rebellion in the Backlands'', 1944), which helped inspire and provide material for the novel ''[[The War of the End of the World]]'' by Peruvian Nobel Laureate [[Mario Vargas Llosa]] (1981). The conflict was also described at length by Peter Robb in "A Death in Brazil" (2004). In fictional form it appears in the novel "[[The First Garment]]" (1975) by a [[Georgians|Georgian]] writer [[Guram Dochanashvili]].
