'''Cuauhtémoc''' ({{IPA|es|kwawˈtemok|-|Cuahutemoc.ogg}}) is a [[Boroughs of Mexico City|borough of Mexico City]]. Named after the 16th-century Aztec ruler [[Cuauhtémoc]], it contains the oldest parts of the city, extending over what was the entire urban core of Mexico City in the 1920s.

Cuauhtémoc is the [[historic center of Mexico City|historic and cultural center of Mexico City]], although it is not the geographical center. While it ranks only sixth in population, it generates about a third of the entire city's GDP, mostly through commerce and services. It is home to the [[Mexican Stock Exchange]], the important tourist attractions of the historic center and [[Zona Rosa, Mexico City|Zona Rosa]], and various skyscrapers, such as the [[Torre Mayor]] and the Mexican headquarters of HSBC. It also contains numerous museums, libraries, government offices, [[Traditional fixed markets in Mexico|markets]], and other commercial centers, which can bring in as many as 5 million people each day to work, shop, or visit cultural sites.

This area has had problems with [[urban decay]], especially in the historic center. Efforts to revitalize the historic center and some other areas have been going on since the 1990s, by both government and private entities. Such efforts have resulted in better public parks, such as the [[Alameda Central]], which was renovated, and the modification of streets such as 16 de Septiembre and [[Madero Street|Madero]] that have become car-free for pedestrians ({{lang|es|zona peatonal}}).

==Description==
[[File:Zócalo cdmx.jpg|thumb|left|View of the Zocalo]]
[[File:Bellas Artes 01.jpg|thumb|left|Palace of Fine Arts]]
Cuauhtémoc is centered on the [[Zócalo]] or main square, which contains the Aztec ruins of the [[Templo Mayor]], the [[Mexico City Cathedral|Metropolitan Cathedral]], and the [[National Palace (Mexico)|National Palace of Mexico]].<ref name="hisdel">{{cite web |url=http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/historia/historia.html |title=Historia de la Delegación |publisher=Borough of Cuauhtémoc |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=History of the Borough |access-date=November 5, 2010 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110515185925/http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/historia/historia.html |archive-date=May 15, 2011 |url-status=dead }}</ref> The borough covers 32.44 km<sup>2</sup>, divided into 34 colonias, 2,627 city blocks, 1,267,000 m<sup>2</sup> of green areas, 1,500 buildings classified as national monuments, two archeological zones (Tlatelolco and Templo Mayor), 1,290 private buildings with official historic value ({{lang|es|valor patrimonial de propiedad privada}}), 210 public buildings with official historic value, 120 government buildings, and two major planned-housing complexes ([[Unidad Habitacional Nonoalco-Tlatelolco]] and [[Centro Urbano Benito Juárez]]).<ref name="gobterr">{{cite web |url=http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/cifras/territorio.html |title=Territorio |publisher=Borough of Cuauhtémoc |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=Territory |access-date=November 5, 2010 |archive-date=February 12, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20120212193912/http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/cifras/territorio.html |url-status=dead }}</ref>

Because it is the oldest part of Mexico City, with buildings that are centuries old, deterioration is an ongoing concern. Currently, at least 789 inhabited buildings in 12 colonias have been listed as in danger of condemnation, due to structural damage caused by sinking into muddy soil of the former lakebed. These are mostly located in the historic center and the colonias immediately surrounding it. Some of these have been classified as having historic or artistic value by the [[Instituto Nacional de Bellas Artes]] or [[Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia]].<ref name="altoriesgo">{{cite news |title=De alto riesgo, la delegación Cuauhtémoc |author=Phenélope Aldaz |url=http://www.eluniversal.com.mx/ciudad/103416.html |newspaper=El Universal |location=Mexico City |date=September 21, 2010 |access-date=November 5, 2010 |language=es |trans-title=At high rist, the Cuauhtémoc borough |archive-date=October 11, 2012 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20121011014430/http://www.eluniversal.com.mx/ciudad/103416.html |url-status=dead }}</ref> This has been a problem for the area for centuries and has involved famous structures such as the Metropolitan Cathedral, which had major foundation work done to stop the damage caused by uneven sinking.<ref name="BBC">{{cite news |first=Peter |last=Greste |title=World: Americas Saving Mexico's sinking cathedral |url=http://news.bbc.co.uk/1/hi/world/americas/250011.stm  |publisher=BBC |date=September 12, 2008 |access-date=September 18, 2008 }}</ref>

A large part of this borough is divided between commercial zones and historic and cultural sites. While the borough does not have the highest crime rate in the city, with 13.9% of all Mexico City crime committed here,<ref name="conflictivas"/><ref name="desecon"/> it was, as of 2007, considered to be fairly dangerous because of its [[urbanization]] and because most of the people found in the borough are there only to work or visit. In some older neighborhoods, people live and raise children along with street vending, squatting, and takeover of public spaces by drug addicts, drug dealers, and prostitutes. As of 2007, seven of the borough's 34 colonias had at one point been ranked in the top 10 most lawless in the city by the secretary of public safety of Mexico City,<ref name="conflictivas"/> with a few, such as [[Tepito]], gaining infamy for being so.<ref name="salgado">{{cite news |title= Tepito: la historia de un barrio donde es caro el "impuesto a la ingenuidad"  |first=Agustin |last=Salgado |url=http://www.jornada.unam.mx/2007/03/16/index.php?section=capital&article=041n1cap |newspaper=La Jornada |location=Mexico City |date=March 16, 2007 |access-date=November 11, 2009 |language=es |trans-title=Tepito:the history of a neighborhood where being naïve is expensive }}</ref> Some of these rundown areas were lower class colonias such as Colonia Guerrero and Colonia Morelos, but similar problems were also found in [[upper middle class]] colonias such as the northern part of [[Colonia Roma]].<ref name="conflictivas"/> The most common crimes were muggings, with 1.47 reports per day, robbery of businesses with .78 reports per day, and car theft with .71 reports per day.<ref name="conflictivas"/>

Most of the 5 million who come into this borough each day are there to work, visit the area's markets, shops, and cultural attractions, or are tourists.<ref name="conflictivas"/> The borough is the most visited area of the city by tourists,<ref name="tercerdel">{{cite news |title=Benito Juárez, tercer delegación más visitada por turistas |author=Ángel Gómez |url=http://www.eluniversaldelvalle.mx/detalle1993.html |newspaper=El Universal |location=Mexico City |date=July 28, 2010 |access-date=November 5, 2010 |language=es |trans-title=Benito Juarez, third most visited by tourists |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110722224726/http://www.eluniversaldelvalle.mx/detalle1993.html |archive-date=July 22, 2011 |url-status=usurped }}</ref> who mostly come to see the historic center and [[Zona Rosa, Mexico City|Zona Rosa]].<ref name="conflictivas"/> People from other parts of the city come to visit the museums and large public markets such as La Lagunilla, Mixcalco, Hidalgo, Medellín, and San Juan. The influx brings in 800,000 vehicles to circulate its streets each day, with traffic jams, especially in and near the historic center nearly a daily occurrence.<ref name="conflictivas"/>

===Demographics===

While it is the most important borough [[economy of Mexico|economically]], bringing millions of people into its territory on any given day,<ref name="conflictivas">{{cite news |title= En la delegación Cuauhtémoc, siete de las 10 colonias más conflictivas del DF |first=Mirna |last=Servin Vega |url= http://www.jornada.unam.mx/2007/07/28/index.php?section=capital&article=035n1cap |newspaper=La Jornada |publisher=[[UNAM]] |location=Mexico City |date=July 28, 2007 |access-date=November 5, 2010 |language=es |trans-title=In the Cuauhtemoc borough, seven of ten of the most conflictive colonias in the Distrito Federal}}</ref> its population of 545,884 in 2020 ranks only sixth out of the city's 16 boroughs.<ref name="census2020" /> This population has been steadily decreasing even as the population of the rest of the city has remained static, although a slight increase occurred in the last 10 years. A fairly large percentage of the population is over 60 years of age and over half of the residents are either single or living with a partner. The borough contains only seven percent of all housing units in the city.

Those who do live here are mostly employed in services (57.5%) and commerce (23.4%).<ref name="desecon">{{cite web |url=http://www.siege.df.gob.mx/estadistico/pdf/monografias/cua.pdf |title=Delegación Cuauhtémoc |publisher=Secretaria de Desarrollo Económico-Government of Mexico City |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=Cuauhtémoc borough |access-date=November 5, 2010 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110722225208/http://www.siege.df.gob.mx/estadistico/pdf/monografias/cua.pdf |archive-date=July 22, 2011 |url-status=dead }}</ref> 

===Government===

The borough was first established on December 30, 1970, after the circumscription of Mexico City was split into four boroughs. Before the political reforms of 2016, it was governed by a borough chief ({{lang|es|jefe delegacional}})) and a cabinet consisting of a ''secretaria particular, coordinacion de asesores, dirección interinstitucional y de fomento economico, subdirección técnica, subdirección de comunicación social,'' and'' subdirección de unidades habitacionales''. After becoming an {{lang|es|alcaldía}}, the head of government became a mayor.<ref name="organigrama">{{cite web |url= http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/directorio/organigramas.html|title= Organigrama |publisher=Borough of Cuauhtémoc |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=Organization map |access-date=November 5, 2010 }}</ref>

The seat of the borough government is located in Colonia Buenavista.

==History==

=== Tenochtitlan ===
{{Main|Tenochtitlan#History}}
[[File:Tlatelolco Marketplace.JPG|thumb|Model of the marketplace of Tlatelolco with the Templo Mayor in the background]]
The early history of the delegation coincides with the [[History of Mexico City|history of Aztec Tenochtitlan and colonial Mexico City]]. [[Tenochitlan]] was founded on a marshy island in [[Lake Texcoco]]. It was divided into four ''capuillis'' or neighborhoods centering on the Templo Mayor. This temple's ruins are located very close to the modern main square or Zocalo today.<ref name="hisdel"/>

=== Spanish rule ===
When the [[Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire|Spanish conquered Tenochtitlan in 1521]], they destroyed most of the old Aztec public buildings, but kept the basic layout of the city, which roughly extends over what is now known as the historic center or Colonia Centro. The cathedral was built over a portion of the sacred precinct (''teocalli'') of the destroyed Templo Mayor, the National Palace was built over [[Moctezuma II|Moctezuma]]'s New Palace, and the Zocalo was built over what was an open space near the sacred temple space. Over the early colonial period, European-style construction replaced Aztec ones over the entire island city, with the most important public buildings concentrated on the blocks adjoining the Zocalo.

As the center of New Spain, the city held the greatest prestige, prompting those who had made their fortunes through conquest, mining, commerce, and other means to have homes in the city, as close to the Zocalo as possible. The city soon became filled with mansions, large churches and monasteries, and monumental public buildings, which would eventually earn it the nickname of "City of Palaces".<ref name="hisdel"/>

At the beginning of the 19th century, this city remained mostly within what is now called the historic center, although various drainage projects had been enlarging the island. The city proper contained 397 streets and alleys, 12 bridges, 78 plazas, 14 parish churches, 41 monasteries, 10 colleges, seven hospitals, a poorhouse, a cigar factory, 19 restaurants, two inns, 28 corrals for horses, and two official neighborhoods.

=== Independence era ===
[[File:Edificioparis.jpg|175px|thumb|19th Century [[Porfirio Diaz|Porfirian]] architecture in [[Colonia Roma]].]]
After Mexico gained its independence in 1824, Mexico City was designated as the capital of the new country, and the city and its surrounding area (11.5 km<sup>2</sup>) were incorporated as a "Federal District," separate from the other states.<ref name="hisdel"/>
By the late, 19th century, the city began to break its traditional confines with the construction of new neighborhoods, called colonias, in the still-drying lakebed. This was especially true in the areas west of the historic area, with the creation of "modern" colonias for the wealthy along the [[Paseo de la Reforma]], built earlier by [[Maximilian I of Mexico|Maximilian I]]. These colonias include [[Colonia Juárez (Mexico City)|Colonia Juárez]], [[Colonia Roma]], [[Colonia Cuauhtémoc, Mexico City|Colonia Cuauhtémoc]], and [[Colonia San Rafael]].<ref name="Lonely">{{cite book  |title=Lonely Planet Mexico City:Your map to the megalopolis  |last=Noble |first=John |year=2000 |publisher=Lonely Planet |location=Oakland CA |isbn=978-1-86450-087-5 }}</ref> Colonias for poorer and working-class people were built mostly north and south of the city such as Colonia Morelos, and [[Colonia Doctores]].<ref name="DelCuauh">{{cite web |url=http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/mapa/colonias.html |title=Colonia Doctores |language=es |access-date=November 4, 2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080810220028/http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/mapa/colonias.html |archive-date=August 10, 2008 }}</ref><ref name="delegmorelos">{{cite web |url=http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/mapa/colonias.html |title=Colonia Morelos |language=es |access-date=November 4, 2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080810220028/http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/mapa/colonias.html |archive-date=August 10, 2008 }}</ref>

=== 20th century ===
In 1928, President [[Álvaro Obregón]] divided the rapidly growing Federal District area into 13 boroughs (''delegaciones''), with what was then the city proper designated as the Cuauhtémoc borough.<ref name="hisdel"/> While the borough still remained the center of city's commerce, politics, academia, and culture during the first half of the 20th century, this historic center began going into decline, as the wealthy moved out into the new western colonias as early as the end of 19th century. By the 1950s, the country's main university, [[UNAM]], moved almost all of its facilities out of the borough and into the newly built [[University City of Mexico|Ciudad Universitaria]] in the south of the city.<ref name="Lonely"/> In the 1940s, the city government froze rents in the borough, and by the late 1990s, when this was finally repealed, many tenants were paying the same prices they were in the 1950s. With no financial incentive to keep up their properties, landlords let their buildings disintegrate.<ref name="belated"/> Most of this occurred in the historic center, but this phenomenon also presented itself in other areas, such as Colonia San Rafael<ref name="lromano">{{cite news |title= San Rafael: Desgastada por el abandono |author=Luz Romano |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=April 9, 2003 |page= 6 |language=es |trans-title=San Rafael: worn out from abandonment }}</ref> and the Centro Urbano Benito Juárez, as well.<ref name="paltridge130">{{Cite thesis |degree=PhD |title= Carlos Merida's integration of art, architecture and ideology in the Multifamiliar Juarez |author=Blair Allen Paltridge |year=1992 |publisher= University of California, Los Angeles |page=130 |docket= AAT 9224132 }}</ref>

Since the 1950s, the city has received the highest number of migrants from other parts of Mexico. Most of these come from very rural areas of the country, and a significant percentage speak an indigenous language with Spanish as a second language, or do not speak Spanish at all. As of 2005, seven percent of the borough's population is made up of these migrants, who have put strains on services such as education.<ref name="exclusion">{{cite web |url=http://www.educacion.df.gob.mx/se//images/exclusion_educativa/cuauhtemoc.pdf |title=Exclusion Educativa en la Delegacion Cuauhtémoc |publisher=Government of Mexico City |year=2009 |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=Educational exclusion in the Cuauhtemoc borough |access-date=November 5, 2010 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110722224939/http://www.educacion.df.gob.mx/se//images/exclusion_educativa/cuauhtemoc.pdf |archive-date=July 22, 2011 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

Between the flight of wealthier residents from the historic center and the colonias that immediately surround it, and the damage from the 1985 earthquake, parts of the borough became deserted at night.<ref name="belated" /> Former mansions had been converted into tenements for the poor,<ref name="Lonely"/><ref name="facelift" /><ref name="jcorona">{{Cite news|title= Es Tlaxcoaque zona olvidada |first=Juan |last=Corona |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=November 8, 2009 |page=5 |language=es |trans-title=Tlaxcoaque is a forgotten zone }}</ref> and the sidewalks and streets were taken over by [[pickpocket]]s and street vendors, especially in the historic center.<ref name="belated"/><ref name="facelift"/> This made the area undesirable for tourists. As the historic center is the city's main tourist attraction, the city lost its standing as a destination for international visitors, instead becoming an airport connection for other areas of the country.<ref name="facelift">{{cite journal |last=Smith |first=Geri |date=May 2004 |title=Mexico City gets a face-lift |journal=Business Week |issue=3884 |id=00077135 |url=https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2004-05-23/mexico-city-gets-a-face-lift}}</ref> Until recently, many of the restaurants of the area, even the best, would close early to allow employees time to get home because the area was not particularly safe at night.<ref name="newface">{{cite journal |last=Butler |first=Ron |date=Nov–Dec 1999 |title=A New Face for the Zocalo |journal=Americas |volume=51 |issue=6 |pages=4–6 |id=03790940 }}</ref>

=== Contemporary events ===
Starting in the late 1990s, the city and federal governments, along with some private associations, have worked to revitalize the borough, especially the historic center. Starting in the early 2000s, the government infused 500&nbsp;million [[Mexican pesos|pesos]] (US$55&nbsp;million) into the Historic Center Trust<ref name="belated"/> and entered into a partnership with a business group led by [[Carlos Slim]], to buy dozens of centuries-old buildings and other real estate to rehabilitate.<ref name="facelift"/> Work has concentrated on renovating historic buildings, repaving streets, and improving water, lighting and other infrastructure.<ref name="belated"/><ref name="Jornada">{{cite news|first=Cruz |last=Alejandro |title=Arrasa con vestigios prehispánicos rescate del Centro Histórico |url=http://www.jornada.unam.mx/ultimas/2008/08/30/arrasan-con-vestigios-prehispanicos-obras-urbanas |publisher=La Jornada |date=August 30, 2008 |access-date=September 2, 2008 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080901214403/http://www.jornada.unam.mx/ultimas/2008/08/30/arrasan-con-vestigios-prehispanicos-obras-urbanas |archive-date=September 1, 2008 }}</ref> A number of the oldest streets near the Zocalo have been made pedestrian-only and most street vendors have been forced to move out of the historic center.<ref name="Lonely"/> This paved the way for the opening of upscale eateries, bars, and fashionable stores.<ref name="Lonely"/> Also, young people are moving into downtown lofts. To attract more tourists, new red double-decker buses are now in use.<ref name="facelift"/> Other efforts have been made in other parts of the borough, such as in Colonia Juarez and Colonia Obrera, but with mixed results.<ref name="cdmex">{{cite web |url= http://www.mexicocity-guide.com/zones/coloniajuarez.htm |title= Colonia Juárez |publisher= Mexico City-Guide.com |location= Mexico City |language= es |access-date= September 15, 2010 |archive-url= https://web.archive.org/web/20101030043718/http://www.mexicocity-guide.com/zones/coloniajuarez.htm |archive-date= October 30, 2010 |url-status= dead }}</ref><ref name="buscan">{{cite web|url=http://www.arts-history.mx/banco/index.php?id_nota=10092008162526 |title=Buscan en la colonia obrera alternativas de vivienda económica |author=CONACULTA |author-link=CONACULTA |work=Artes e Historia |location=Mexico |language=es |trans-title=Looking for alternative affordable housing in Colonia Obrera |access-date=July 11, 2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110719094606/http://www.arts-history.mx/banco/index.php?id_nota=10092008162526 |archive-date=July 19, 2011 }}</ref>

This has not resolved all of the borough's problems, though. Many of the problems with urban decay (abandoned buildings, squatters, uncontrolled street vending, crime, etc.) continue.<ref name="rico">{{cite news |title= Tepito, barrio bravo de México |first=Maite |last=Rico |url= http://www.elpais.com/articulo/internacional/Tepito/barrio/bravo/Mexico/elpepuint/20060621elpepiint_10/Tes |newspaper=El País |location=Madrid |date=June 21, 2006 |access-date=November 11, 2009 |language=es |trans-title=Tepito, fierce neighborhood of Mexico }}</ref><ref name="culpan">{{cite news |title= Culpan a la Doctores |author=Ricardo Zamora |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=October 5, 2001 |page=2 |language=es |trans-title=They blame Colonia Doctores }}</ref><ref name="rtrece">{{cite news |title=Marchan en defensa de los derechos de la mujer |url=http://www.radiotrece.com.mx/nota.php?id=13810 |newspaper=Radio Trece |location=Mexico City |date=September 28, 2010 |access-date=October 12, 2010 |language=es |trans-title=March in defense of the rights of women |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110722224953/http://www.radiotrece.com.mx/nota.php?id=13810 |archive-date=July 22, 2011 }}</ref> The continued migration of people into the area from rural parts of Mexico has increased illiteracy rates, in addition to poorer areas, where dropout rates are high.<ref name="exclusion"/> The borough government has been accused of corruption by the Cámara de Comercio, Servicios y Turismo (Chamber of Commerce, Services and Tourism), especially in the issuance of business permits with exorbitant fees and fines. Most of the problem is with small ''torilla'' operations, paper stores. and small grocery stores. which operate completely at the discretion of borough agents.<ref name="corrupcion">{{cite news |title=Denuncian corrupción en la delegación Cuauhtémoc |author=Georgina Pineda |url=http://impreso.milenio.com/node/8523584 |archive-url=https://archive.today/20130128163605/http://impreso.milenio.com/node/8523584 |url-status=dead |archive-date=January 28, 2013 |newspaper=Milenio |location=Mexico City |date=January 23, 2009 |access-date=November 5, 2010 |language=es |trans-title=Denounce corruption in Cuauhtemoc }}</ref>

==Economy==
[[File:15-07-12-Ciclistas-en-Mexico-RalfR-N3S 8977.jpg|thumb|150px|left|Angel of Independence in Paseo de la Reforma]]
Cuauhtémoc alone accounts for 35.1% of Mexico City's entire GDP,<ref name="desecon"/> and by itself, has the seventh-largest economy in Mexico.<ref name="conflictivas"/> Most of the borough's economy is based on commerce (52.2%), followed by services (39.4%).<ref name="desecon"/> The borough is home to a large number of federal and city government buildings, especially in the historic center, [[Colonia Tabacalera]], and Colonia Doctores.<ref name="deterioro">{{cite news |title=La Tabacalera,una centuria de deterioro |author=Arturo Paramo |url=http://www.inehrm.gob.mx/pdf/exc_img_asicomenzo.pdf |newspaper=Excelsior |location=Mexico City |date=November 21, 2008 |page=4 |access-date=October 26, 2010 |language=es |trans-title=Tabacalera, a century of deterioration |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20101231201016/http://www.inehrm.gob.mx/pdf/exc_img_asicomenzo.pdf |archive-date=December 31, 2010 }}</ref><ref name="delegacion"/>

[[Paseo de la Reforma]], especially the section that divides Colonia Juárez from Colonia Cuauhtémoc, is the most modern and constantly developing part of the borough. It is home to the Mexican Stock Exchange, the headquarters of HSBC in Mexico, and Mexico City's tallest skyscrapers, [[Torre Reforma]], [[Chapultepec Uno R509|Chapultepec Uno]], [[Torre BBVA México]], and [[Torre Mayor]].

Construction of office buildings and high-rise apartments continue in the area, causing it to become a distinctive neighborhood of its own; the high-rises that face the avenue are very distinct from the older ones behind them, mostly used for more traditional housing and small businesses. One of the newest major projects in the mid-2000s was Reforma 222, two towers combining office space with residential units.<ref name="delegacion">{{cite web |url=http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/mapa/colonias.html |title=Colonia Cuautémoc |publisher=Borough of Cuauhtémoc |location=Mexico City |language=es |access-date=September 1, 2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20080810220028/http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/mapa/colonias.html |archive-date=August 10, 2008 }}</ref><ref name="pequeño">{{cite news |title= Hacen 'pequeno barrio'|author= Jesus Alberto Hernandez |newspaper= Reforma  |location=Mexico City |date=August 25, 2002 |language=es  |page=6 |trans-title=They make a "small neighborhood" }}</ref><ref name="cumple">{{cite news |title=Cumple un siglo la Juarez historico |author= Alejandra Bordon.  |newspaper=Reforma |location=Mexico City |date=March 3, 2006 |page=2 |language=es |trans-title=Colonia Juarez has 100th birthday }}</ref>
[[File:FoodVendorsAbRodMktDF.JPG|thumb|150px|Food vendors in the Abelardo L. Rodriguez Market]]
In the rest of the borough, commerce is more traditional, with numerous public markets, informal markets called ''[[tianguis]]'', and street peddling. Public markets are buildings constructed and maintained by a city or municipal government, which rents stands to private vendors. The largest is [[La Lagunilla Market, Mexico City|La Lagunilla Market]], with nearly 2,000 vendors divided among three large warehouse-type buildings. It is known for its large furniture and shoe markets, but most of the vendors sell food and everyday items.<ref name="salvar">{{cite news |title= Intentan salvar La Lagunilla  |author= Ernesto Osorio |newspaper= Reforma  |location=Mexico City |date=May 3, 2010 |language=es  |page=2 |trans-title=They try to save La Lagunilla}}</ref><ref name="gobmercado">{{cite web |url= http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/atracciones/lugares/mercados.html |title= Mercados |publisher=Borough of Cuauhtémoc |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=Markets |access-date=November 5, 2010 }}</ref> Designed to "modernize" the tradition of ''tianguis'' or street markets, some were even promoted through art, such as the [[Abelardo L. Rodriguez Market, Mexico City|Abelardo L. Rodriguez Market]].<ref name="Gomez">{{cite news|first=Laura |last=Gomez Florez |title= Remodelan el histórico mercado Abelardo L. Rodríguez como parte del rescate del Centro |url=http://www.jornada.unam.mx/2008/05/19/index.php?section=capital&article=039n1cap  |publisher=La Jornada |location=Mexico City |date=May 19, 2008 |access-date=June 3, 2009 |language=es}}</ref> ''Tianguis'' still survive and can be found in most parts of the borough, as well as in much of the rest of Mexico.<ref name="cinformal">{{cite news |title= El Comercio Informal: entre negocio y cultura |first=Gabriel |last= Orihuela |newspaper=Mural |location=Guadalajara, Mexico |date=February 12, 2001 |page=1 |language=es |trans-title=The Informal Economy: Between business and culture }}</ref> In the borough, the best known tianguis are located in [[Tepito]], where 12,000 people do business on the streets.<ref name="rico"/>

Tourism plays a major role in the borough's economy. It contains some of the best-known landmarks of Mexico City, so it has become the most visited area of the city by tourists. The most popular areas are the historic center, Alameda Central/Bellas Artes, Reforma, and Zona Rosa.<ref name="conflictivas"/><ref name="tercerdel"/> The borough also has the most developed hotel infrastructure, with 389 of the 6,464 hotels in the city. About half of all four- and five-star hotels are located here.<ref name="desecon"/>

Government is also one of the main employers; the [[National Palace (Mexico)|National Government Palace]], the [[Senate of the Republic (Mexico)|Senate of the Republic]] and [[Mexico City administrative buildings]] are located in the borough.

==Transportation==
===Roads===
[[File:METROBUS EN SERVICIO.jpg|thumb|Metrobus on Avenida Insurgentes, Colonia Tabacalera]]
The borough has 14 million meters of roadways with 314 main intersections. The vast majority of these are current and former residential streets, but three expressways and various axis roads ({{lang|es|ejes viales}}) are available for through traffic.<ref name="vialidades">{{cite web |url= http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/cifras/vialidades.html |title= Vialidades |publisher=Borough of Cuauhtémoc |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=Roadways |access-date=November 5, 2010 }}</ref>

The three main arteries are the [[Circuito Interior]], [[Viaducto Miguel Alemán]], and San Antonio Abad, which were built for traffic passing through the center of the city. The Circuito Interior, a circular bypass, is the second-most important roadway in the city after the [[Anillo Periférico]].<ref name="atoron">{{cite news |title= Sufre Circuito atorón |author= Sergio Fimbres |newspaper= Reforma|location=Mexico City |date=January 10, 2008 |language=es  |page=1 |trans-title=Circuito suffers major traffic jam}}</ref>

The {{lang|es|ejes viales}} are a series of north-south and west-east roads built by [[Carlos Hank González (politician)|Carlos Hank González]] in the 1980s to make Mexico City more automobile-friendly.<ref name="vialidades"/> The largest of these is the [[Eje Central]], which runs north south and divides the historic center in half. Due to the large number of people who enter and leave this borough each day, up to 800,000 vehicles circulate the streets each day, making traffic jams, especially in the historic center, a frequent occurrence.<ref name="conflictivas"/>

===Bicitaxis===
The [[cycle rickshaw]], known in Mexico as {{lang|es|bicitaxi}} (from the English "bike taxi"), is a popular means of transport in the historic center.<ref>{{cite web|url=https://mexico.quadratin.com.mx/bicitaxis-tandems-opciones-lugar-micros-zocalo-mancera/|title=Bicitaxis y tándems, opciones en lugar de micros en Zócalo: Mancera|work=Quadratin CDMX|author=Marco Antonio|date=2016-08-16|access-date=2021-02-24|language=es}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.cyclecity.com.mx/los-bictaxis-en-el-centro-historico/|title=Los bicitaxis en el Centro Histórico|work=Cycle City|author=Ramón Arizmendi and Óscar Martínez Corona|date=2018-06-07|access-date=2021-02-24|language=es|archive-date=April 21, 2021|archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20210421232348/https://www.cyclecity.com.mx/los-bictaxis-en-el-centro-historico/|url-status=dead}}</ref><ref>{{cite web|url=https://www.elsoldemexico.com.mx/metropoli/cdmx/rescatan-a-bicitaxis-del-centro-historico-4911622.html|title=Rescatan a bicitaxis del Centro Histórico|work=El sol de México|author=Israel Zamarrón|date=2020-03-02|access-date=2021-02-23|language=es}}</ref>

===Public transportation===
The borough has the largest number of [[Mexico City Metro|Metro]] lines running through it.<ref name="conflictivas"/> These include [[Mexico City Metro Line 1|Line 1]], [[Mexico City Metro Line 2|Line 2]], [[Mexico City Metro Line 3|Line 3]], [[Mexico City Metro Line 5|Line 5]], [[Mexico City Metro Line 8|Line 8]], [[Mexico City Metro Line 9|Line 9]], and [[Mexico City Metro Line B|Line B]]. There are also [[Servicio de Transportes Eléctricos|trolleybus]] lines running north-south and east-west, Lines 1 and 3 of the [[Mexico City Metrobús|Metrobus]] as well as numerous bus routes.<ref name="transportes">{{cite web |url= http://www.cuauhtemoc.df.gob.mx/delegacion/cifras/transportes.html |title= Transportes |publisher=Borough of Cuauhtémoc |location=Mexico City |language=es |trans-title=Transportation |access-date=November 5, 2010 }}</ref> Another important public transportation service is the [[Tren Suburbano]] commuter railway, which has its southern terminal in [[Colonia Buenavista]] with service north as far as [[Cuautitlán]] in the [[State of Mexico]].<ref name="metrobuen">{{cite web|url=http://www.metro.df.gob.mx/red/estacion.html?id=147 |title=Metro Buenavista |publisher=Mexico City Metro |location=Mexico City |language=es |access-date=August 10, 2010 |url-status=dead |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20110614004953/http://www.metro.df.gob.mx/red/estacion.html?id=147 |archive-date=June 14, 2011 }}</ref>

==Education==
[[File:UrquizoSchoolDoctores.JPG|thumb|{{lang|es|Escuela Secundaria Diurna No. 102 General Francisco L. Urquizo}} in [[Colonia Doctores]]]]
In the 2007–2008 school year, there were 22,651 K-12 students, about 6.36% of Mexico City's total.  70.64% attend public schools and 29.14% attend private schools.<ref name="exclusion"/> There are 264 public and private preschools, 116 middle schools, 102 technical and regular high schools, and 13 teachers’ colleges.<ref name="gobedu"/> The borough has the lowest level of illiteracy and the highest percentage of students who have finished primary and middle schools, in part because there is a higher than average percentage of private schools, which tend to have better results. The dropout rate, at 7.54%, is better than average.<ref name="exclusion"/>

In 2009, there were 1,737 students receiving special education full or part-time accounting for 4.66% of all [[special education]] students in Mexico City. Over 97% of these students get their services from public institutions. However, the borough has limited facilities for adult education, especially for those who do not speak Spanish as their first language or are undereducated. This is problematic due to the influx of indigenous people from rural parts of Mexico.<ref name="exclusion"/>
