'''Cândido Godói''' ({{IPA|pt|ˈkɐ̃dʒidu gɔˈdɔj}}) is a municipality of 6,151 inhabitants in the state of [[Rio Grande do Sul]], [[Brazil]] near the [[Argentina|Argentine]] border, famous for the high number of [[twin]]s born there. The twin phenomenon is centered in Linha São Pedro, a small settlement in the city of Cândido Godói, in an ethnically homogeneous population of [[German people|German]] descent.<ref name="Matte">{{Cite journal

== Twinning rate ==
The rate of twin births in Cândido Godói is 10%, significantly higher than the overall 1.8% rate for the state of [[Rio Grande do Sul]].<ref name="Matte" /> This rate is unusual, exceeding the highest observed national [[twin]]ning rate (4.5 to 5% for southwestern Nigeria<ref>{{cite news |url=https://www.bbc.co.uk/worldservice/people/highlights/010607_twins.shtml |title=The Land of Twins: Seeing Double In Igbo-Ora |date=7 June 2001 |work=BBC World Service}}</ref>). Nearly half (8 of 17) of twins examined in one study were [[monozygotic]] (identical) twins, a few more than the average of 30%.<ref name="Matte" /><!--- i.e. not a significant difference, despite the NYT ---> Twin births were noted from the early twentieth century, when the first immigrants included seventeen sets of twins, and have been observed through several generations in the latter part of the twentieth century.<ref name="Natgeo">{{cite web |url=http://channel.nationalgeographic.com/series/explorer/4087/facts |title=Nazi Mystery:Twins from Brazil |publisher=National Geographic Explorer |access-date=2009-11-30 |archive-url=https://web.archive.org/web/20100520033349/http://channel.nationalgeographic.com/series/explorer/4087/facts |archive-date=2010-05-20 |url-status=dead }}</ref>

The population is largely of Polish or German ancestry, with many tracing ancestry to the [[Hunsrück]] region of Germany, which has a higher than average twinning rate.<ref name="Natgeo" /> The rate in Cândido Godói could reflect genetic [[founder effect]]: rare genetic traits occurring by chance among a small group founding a community will be more common among their descendants than in the population at large.<ref>{{Cite journal

=== Explanation attempts ===

The notorious [[Nazi]] doctor [[Josef Mengele]], who had conducted twin "studies" in Germany and experiments with twins in [[Auschwitz]], is known to have fled to South America as the [[Allies of World War II|Allies]] were closing in on the [[Nazi Germany|Nazi German regime]]. The Argentine historian Jorge Camarasa has suggested that Mengele conducted experiments on women in the area, which could be responsible for the high ratio of twins.<ref>{{cite news

}}</ref> According to some commentators, about the time of Mengele's arrival in southern Brazil in 1963, the incidence of [[twins]] began to increase, allegedly leading to the current rate of twinning at 1 in 10, over half of whom are dizygotic (fraternal).<ref name="Matte"/><ref name="NYTimes">{{cite news
 | url = https://www.nytimes.com/2009/02/23/world/americas/23twins.html?_r=1&scp=1&sq=CANDIDO%20GODOI&st=cse

Moreover, according to geneticists, the most likely explanation for the high frequency of twins is genetic isolation and inbreeding.<ref name="Matte"/><ref name="NYTimes"/><ref>{{cite news

This phenomenon of a large number of twin births is not unique to Cândido Godói, and has also been observed in the town of [[Igbo-Ora]] in [[Nigeria]] and the village of [[Kodinhi]] in [[India]].
